# Under the Scope with Pastor Billy Daws
## Series: The Unseen Hand — Joseph (Genesis 37–50)
## Episode 1 — "The Dreamer and the Coat"

**Runtime target:** 22–26 min
**Title + 2 alternates:** "The Dreamer and the Coat" · "When Your Own Family
Turns on You" · "The Pit Before the Palace"
**One-line premise:** A favored son with a God-given dream is hated, stripped,
and sold by his own brothers — and the very moment his life looks finished is the
moment God's unseen hand starts to move.
**Passage(s):** Genesis 37 (NKJV). Anchor verse: Genesis 37:20.
**Bridge verses:** Genesis 37:3–4, 37:11; Romans 8:28.
**Guest:** None.

## COLD OPEN (0:00–0:30)

Have you ever had a day where the bottom just dropped out — and you were sure
that was the end of your story?

Picture a seventeen-year-old boy. His father's favorite. A dreamer. And in one
afternoon his own brothers strip the coat off his back, throw him in a pit, and
sell him to strangers headed for a foreign country. Gone. As far as anybody back
home knew, he was dead.

But that pit wasn't the end of his story. It was the beginning of it. Today we're
putting Joseph under the scope.

## INTRO (0:30–2:15)

I'm Billy Daws, and I'm glad you're here. On this show we take the things of God,
the things of Scripture, the things of everyday life — and we look at them up
close. Not from a distance. Not the way we assume they are. Up close, where you
can see what's really there.

And today we're starting something I've been looking forward to for a long time.
Six episodes walking through one of the greatest stories in the whole Bible — the
life of Joseph, Genesis 37 through 50. If you've ever felt like life took
something from you that you can't get back — if you've ever wondered whether God
is even paying attention when things fall apart — this series is for you.

Because Joseph's story is about a hand you can't always see. God's hand. Moving
in the dark, moving through other people's sin, moving through years of waiting —
and working the whole time. We're going to hold that under the scope, one chapter
at a time.

But every great story has a first page. And Joseph's first page is not a
victory. It's a betrayal. So let's start where the Bible starts him — at home,
with his family, with a coat. Let's unpack this.

## SEGMENT 1 — The favorite son and the coat (2:15–8:30)

**Key point:** Favoritism planted a seed of hatred — and Joseph walked into that
storm without seeing it coming, but God did.

Let's set the scene. Joseph is seventeen. He's one of twelve sons of Jacob — the
man God renamed Israel. And right away, Genesis 37 tells us something that's
going to color this whole chapter. Verse 3, NKJV:

> "Now Israel loved Joseph more than all his children, because he was the son of
> his old age. Also he made him a tunic of many colors."

The son of his old age. The favorite. And Jacob doesn't keep it quiet — he puts
it on display. A tunic of many colors. You've probably heard it called the coat
of many colors. That coat was a statement. In a working family, the other boys
are out in the fields sweating, and here's Joseph in a long, ornate robe that
says, *"This is the special one."*

Now put yourself in the brothers' shoes for just a second. Every time that coat
walks by, it says what Dad thinks of them. And look what it does. Verse 4:

> "But when his brothers saw that their father loved him more than all his
> brothers, they hated him and could not speak peaceably to him."

They couldn't speak a kind word to him. Not one. That's a home under a slow boil.
And I want to be honest under the scope here — because this is a Bible full of
real people, not perfect ones. Jacob was a godly man in many ways, but showing
favoritism was a sin, and it did real damage. Scripture doesn't hide that. It
lets us see it, so we'll learn from it.

Here's the thing about a house like that: everybody feels it, and nobody says
it. The resentment just grows underground. Like a root you can't see, working its
way under the foundation of that family. And Joseph — young, maybe a little
unwise, wearing the coat, bringing back a bad report on his brothers — Joseph is
walking around not fully seeing the storm building around him.

But somebody sees it. God sees it. And this is the first thing I want you to hold
onto today: nothing in Joseph's life is happening outside of God's sight. Not the
favoritism. Not the hatred. Not what's coming. It looks like a family quietly
falling apart. And underneath it, God is already at work.

Hold that — because the story is about to get worse before it gets better.

---

## SEGMENT 2 — The dreams (8:30–15:30)

**Key point:** God gave Joseph a real dream — a promise — and the dream is what
his brothers hated most. But you can hate a promise; you can't stop it.

Now into this house that's already a powder keg, God drops a spark. Joseph has a
dream. Verse 5:

> "Now Joseph had a dream, and he told it to his brothers; and they hated him
> even more."

He tells them the dream. Verse 7 — they're all binding sheaves of grain out in
the field:

> "There we were, binding sheaves in the field. Then behold, my sheaf arose and
> also stood upright; and indeed your sheaves stood all around and bowed down to
> my sheaf."

You don't need a seminary degree to catch what that means. Your sheaves bowed
down to mine. And the brothers catch it too. Verse 8:

> "And his brothers said to him, 'Shall you indeed reign over us? Or shall you
> indeed have dominion over us?' So they hated him even more for his dreams and
> for his words."

Then he has a second dream — the sun, the moon, and eleven stars bowing down.
And this time even his father rebukes him. Look at verse 11, and this is a small
line but don't miss it:

> "And his brothers envied him, but his father kept the matter in mind."

His brothers envied him — but his father kept it in mind. Jacob tucked it away.
Because down deep, Jacob wondered if there was something to it.

Now let's put these dreams under the scope, because this is important. These
weren't just a teenager's ego running wild. This was God. God was giving Joseph a
genuine glimpse of the future — a promise that one day these very brothers would
bow before him. And here's the painful part: the promise is exactly what makes
them hate him.

Have you ever noticed that? Sometimes the thing God has spoken over your life is
the thing that draws the most fire. Joseph's brothers didn't just resent the
coat. They resented the calling. "This dreamer," they start calling him. Like it's
an insult. Like a God-given dream is a joke.

But here's what I want you to see — write this down if you're taking notes.
Nobody in this story can stop that dream. They can hate it. They can mock it.
They're about to try to bury it in a pit. But that dream came from God, and what
God promises, God performs. The brothers thought they were arguing with Joseph.
They were really arguing with God — and that's an argument nobody wins.

So keep that dream in your mind, because in the very next scene, it's going to
look like the dream just died.

---

## SEGMENT 3 — The pit and the sale (15:30–21:30)

**Key point:** At the exact moment Joseph's life looks over, God's unseen hand is
already moving him toward the very throne the dream promised.

Jacob sends Joseph out to check on his brothers, who are off tending flocks. And
watch how fast this turns. Verse 18:

> "Now when they saw him afar off, even before he came near them, they conspired
> against him to kill him."

Before he even reaches them. They see that coat on the horizon, and they start
plotting murder. Verse 19 and 20:

> "Then they said to one another, 'Look, this dreamer is coming!'... 'Come
> therefore, let us now kill him and cast him into some pit; and we shall say,
> "Some wild beast has devoured him." We shall see what will become of his
> dreams!'"

There it is. There's the anchor verse of this whole episode: *"We shall see what
will become of his dreams."* Say it with a sneer, because that's how they meant
it. They think they're about to prove the dream was nothing. They think this is
the end of it.

Church, put that line under the scope, because it is dripping with irony. They
say, "We shall see what will become of his dreams" — as if killing him ends the
story. But God is going to take that exact sentence and answer it. We *will* see
what becomes of his dreams. And it's going to be everything Joseph said and more.
The brothers just don't know they're helping it come true.

Now Reuben talks them out of murder — throw him in the pit instead, he figures
he'll come back and rescue him later. So they grab Joseph. Verse 23:

> "So it came to pass, when Joseph had come to his brothers, that they stripped
> Joseph of his tunic, the tunic of many colors that was on him."

They take the coat. Of course they take the coat — the coat was the whole
problem. And verse 24:

> "Then they took him and cast him into a pit. And the pit was empty; there was no
> water in it."

Sit in that pit with him for a second. Seventeen years old. In the dark. His own
brothers up above eating their lunch while he's down there. I don't think Joseph
had a single verse in that moment that told him how this ends. All he knew was the
dark and the dirt walls. That's as low as it gets.

And then a caravan comes by. Verse 28:

> "Then Midianite traders passed by; so the brothers pulled Joseph up and lifted
> him out of the pit, and sold him to the Ishmaelites for twenty shekels of
> silver."

Twenty pieces of silver. That's the price of his own family. They dip the coat in
goat's blood, carry it home, and let their father believe a wild beast tore him
apart. And the chapter ends — verse 36 — with Joseph sold in Egypt to a man named
Potiphar, an officer of Pharaoh.

Now here's where I want to slow all the way down. Because if you only read it as a
tragedy, you miss the whole point. Look at where that caravan was headed. Egypt.
Look at who bought him. Potiphar — an officer of Pharaoh, right up next to the
throne. The brothers meant to get rid of Joseph. But God used their sin, their
silver, and that passing caravan to move Joseph one step closer to the very
palace where the dream would come true.

Hundreds of years later the Bible looks back on this exact moment. Psalm 105
says, "He sent a man before them — Joseph — who was sold as a slave." *He sent.*
God sent. The brothers thought they were selling him. Heaven says God was sending
him. Same event. Two hands on it. And that's the truth this whole series is built
on — but I'm not going to give you the full weight of it yet. That's for the end
of this journey. For now, just hold this: the pit was not the end. The pit was
the road.

---

## APPLICATION / TAKEAWAY (21:30–24:00)

So what do we carry out of Genesis 37? Let me give you three things, quick.

One — **when you're in the pit, you're not out of the story.** You may be in the
part where God works in the dark. Joseph couldn't see the palace from the bottom
of that pit. Neither can you, right now, in your dark place. But not being able to
see the hand does not mean the hand isn't moving. It is.

Two — **don't let people bury your dream; only God gets to define it.** They can
take your coat. They can mock what God spoke over you. They cannot cancel it.
What God promises, God performs — on His timeline, not theirs. Keep pressing on.

Three — **start learning to trace the hand.** This is the muscle this whole series
is going to build. When something evil happens, ask the harder question: what
might God be doing underneath it? Not because the evil is good — it isn't. But
because our God is the kind of God who can send a man to Egypt in the back of a
slave caravan. Romans 8:28, NKJV:

> "And we know that all things work together for good to those who love God, to
> those who are the called according to His purpose."

*All* things. Even the pit. Even the silver. That's not a slogan you slap on a
wound — that's the God who was with Joseph in the dark. And He's with you.
