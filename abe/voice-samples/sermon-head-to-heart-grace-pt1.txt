Why does our heart lag behind? Why is it that even after years of knowing we're forgiven, the heart still refuses to let go of guilt? The Apostle Paul addresses this very issue in Romans 8, verse 1. He declares with the authority of heaven there is therefore now no condemnation to them which are in Christ Jesus, who walk not after the flesh, but after the Spirit. What a powerful truth that is. But let's break it down. Notice that Paul doesn't say there is therefore now no feelings of guilt. He says, no condemnation. There's a difference, isn't there? Condemnation is a legal term. It's the gavel coming down with a verdict of guilty, but in Christ that verdict has been overturned by the judge of all the earth. Yet our hearts often struggle to accept this acquittal. Part of this struggle is rooted in the human condition. We live in a society that conditions us from an early age to believe that actions have consequences and that forgiveness has to be earned. It's not surprising that we have a hard time accepting something as lavish and scandalous as grace. Unearned, unmerited favor. Grace disrupts the normal flow of human interactions. It defies logic and worldly justice. It's a divine paradox. We are guilty yet declared innocent. And that's a hard pill for the heart to swallow, especially when it's been trained otherwise. Think about the stories we tell ourselves and the narratives ingrained in us from childhood. The world often operates on a system of merit where worth is tied to performance and acceptance is earned. This mindset can seep into our spiritual lives, causing a dissonance between the head and the heart. Have you ever noticed how quickly we accept blame and punishment, but struggle with the idea of unconditional love and forgiveness? It's as if our hearts have built walls to protect us from disappointment even when it's God's love trying to break through. Another layer of this struggle comes from the enemy of our souls, Satan, the accuser. Revelation 12, 10 calls him the accuser of our brethren. He thrives on our doubt, whispering lies about our worthiness, replaying our sins in high definition, hoping we'll forget the cross and focus on the crime. His goal is to keep us bound in a cycle of shame and regret, unable to move fully, fully into the freedom Christ offers. But here's the catch, accepting grace is a process. It's often not instantaneous. The mind may grasp the concept, but the heart takes time to digest it. Think of it like planting a seed. You don't see the fruit immediately, it takes time. The heart needs to soak in the truth of grace to let it settle in and take root. And this is where we need to exercise faith. The same faith that brought us to Christ is the faith that will help our hearts catch up with our heads. It's daily renewing our minds as Paul instructs in Romans 12, 2. Letting the truth of God's word wash over us until the heart finally aligns with what the mind knows we are forgiven. We are free and there is no condemnation. In practical terms, what does this look like? It means speaking truth over yourself, even when you don't feel it. It's declaring, I am forgiven when the accuser comes knocking. It's reminding yourself that your identity is not in what you did, but in what Christ has done. It's surrounding yourself with the community of believers who will speak life into you when your heart feels heavy. It's diving into the word, letting its truth penetrate the heart's defenses. And finally, it's prayer, raw honest prayer, laying your heart bare before God and asking him to bridge the gap. Consider the impact of immersing yourself in the stories of grace found in the Bible. Reflect on the prodigal son who returned home expecting rejection but found open arms. Let these stories be the bomb that softens the heart, allowing it to open up to the possibility that such grace is available to us too. The Bible is replete with examples of God's relentless love and do the same in us. So let's not be discouraged if our hearts are slow to catch up. Let's instead lean into the process, trusting that He who began a good work in us will carry it on to completion. Remember, grace is not doctrine to be understood. It's a reality to be experienced. And it starts with letting it let God's love permeate every corner of our hearts one day at a time. As we engage in the daily practice of grace, we begin to see transformation. The heart in its own time will come to rest in the truth that we are irrevocably loved and forgiven. What a journey that is from head to heart, from knowing, to feeling. Let's embrace it with a whole spirit to guide us through the complexities of our emotions and into the simplicity of his grace.
